Did anyone back then actually read the cycle.
Nobody has been able to show it, and one famous attempt is the reason people assume otherwise.
In the nineteen seventies a researcher named Alexander Marshack looked at scratch marks on Ice Age bone and antler.
He read them as notation systems, specifically lunar calendars, notating the passage of time.
Individual lines and dots as days.
Sequences of them as months.
It is a beautiful idea and it has never gone away.
It has also never been proven.
In 1989 another researcher published a reply in Current Anthropology.
The title of it was Palaeolithic Lunar Calendars, a Case of Wishful Thinking.
That is the whole argument, in five words, before the paper even starts.
The broader criticism is that the work over-interpreted many artifacts, finding numerical and calendrical patterns where none exist.
Which is the polite way of saying that if a person wants to find a moon in a row of scratches, a row of scratches will usually oblige.
So the marks are real.
Somebody made them on purpose, and that much nobody argues with.
Whether they were cut one at a time over many nights, or in a single sitting, is part of what is in dispute.
And it matters, because a tally kept across a month and a pattern carved in an afternoon are not the same object.
What they were counting, if they were counting, is not recoverable.
It may have been nights.
It may have been kills, or debts, or nothing at all.
