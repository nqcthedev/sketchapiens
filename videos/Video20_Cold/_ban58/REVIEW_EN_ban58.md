# REVIEW BẢN TIẾNG ANH — ĐỐI CHIẾU VỚI BẢN VIỆT

Bản tiếng Việt bên dưới là **nguồn chuẩn**. Nó đã qua ba vòng kiểm bằng chứng, một vòng nghe lạnh,
một vòng soát chữ, và chủ kênh đã nghe duyệt. **Nội dung, cấu trúc và độ dài đều đã chốt.**

Bản tiếng Anh là bản đọc thật sẽ vào video. Hai bản **khớp từng dòng**: dòng 1 tiếng Anh ứng với
dòng 1 tiếng Việt, và cứ thế tới dòng 207.

## Tìm đúng sáu thứ

**1. Lệch nghĩa so với bản Việt**
Dòng nào bản Anh **nói thêm**, **nói bớt**, hoặc **nói khác** bản Việt. Chỉ ra số dòng, trích cả hai.

**2. Fact, số, ẩn dụ hoặc claim mới do bản dịch tự thêm**
Đây là thứ tôi lo nhất. Bản Anh không được có bất cứ thứ gì mà bản Việt không có.
Đặc biệt soi mọi **con số** và mọi **tên riêng**.

⚠️ Có một loại khác biệt **cố ý**, đừng báo là lỗi: **đơn vị đo đã đổi sang hệ Mỹ** cho khán giả
Mỹ. `21 độ C` → `seventy degrees` · `một mét hai` → `about four feet` · `hai mét mốt` →
`close to seven feet` · `hơn hai trăm mét dưới mực nước biển` → `more than six hundred feet`.
Nếu thấy chỗ quy đổi nào **sai số học**, đó mới là lỗi cần báo.

**3. Câu nghe như bản dịch**
Câu nào đọc lên thấy cấu trúc tiếng Việt còn nằm bên dưới, thay vì nghe như một người viết thẳng
bằng tiếng Anh. Trích nguyên văn.

**4. Thuật ngữ có đúng chuẩn tiếng Anh không**
`paradoxical undressing` · `isotopes` · `radiation` · `core temperature` · `vessels` ·
`bedding` · `hearth` · `actigraphy / movement trackers`. Nếu có từ nào một kênh khoa học phổ thông
sẽ dùng khác, nói tôi biết.

**5. Ba ràng buộc cứng**
Không dấu chấm than · không gạch ngang giữa câu · mỗi câu một dòng. Báo số dòng nếu phạm.

**6. Câu vấp khi đọc thành tiếng**
Đây là lời đọc, không phải bài viết. Câu nào người đọc phải lấy hơi giữa chừng hoặc đọc lại
mới hiểu.

## Cách trả lời

Bảng: **số dòng · câu tiếng Anh · vấn đề · chữ đề nghị đổi**.

## ⛔ Bốn thứ đừng làm

- **Đừng đề xuất cắt dòng nào.** Độ dài đã chốt, và hai bản phải giữ đúng 207 dòng để khớp
  với bước chia hình phía sau.
- **Đừng gộp hai dòng thành một câu**, kể cả khi tiếng Anh gộp lại thì mượt hơn. Mỗi dòng là
  một khung hình.
- **Đừng bàn cấu trúc, thứ tự hay chuyện bài hay dở.** Xong rồi.
- **Đừng viết lại câu chỉ vì bạn nghĩ có cách hay hơn.** Câu không sai thì để yên.

     1	The sun has just gone down.
     2	Someone is looking for a place to lie down, close enough to the fire to reach it.
     3	There is a whole winter night to get through before it gets light.
     4	And before sleep can come, that person has to finish something they do not know they are doing.

    10	For sixteen years, Michael Rothschild and Volkmar Schneider at the Berlin Institute of Legal Medicine went back through every death by cold they could find.
    11	Sixty nine people.
    12	A quarter of them had taken their clothes off, and this has a name, paradoxical undressing.
    13	And almost every one of those was found somewhere very strange.
    14	Under a bed.
    15	Behind a wardrobe.
    16	Wedged into a shelf.
    17	Nobody put them there.
    18	They crawled in.
    19	The last thing a freezing person does is not lie down and drift off.
    20	It is to find a den and crawl into it, the way an animal does.
    21	Something very old is still running inside us, and in the last minutes it takes over.
    22	If a human body is that fragile in the cold, then twenty thousand years ago, how did anyone lie outside all night and still get up in the morning?
    23	Not on one unlucky night.
    24	Every night, for months, for hundreds of generations.
    25	So let us go back to that night and walk through it from the start.
    26	The answer everyone reaches for is fire.
    27	Fire solves part of it, and we will get there, including the part where it fails.
    28	But the thing that kills you first that night is not even in the air.
    29	It is directly under your back.
    30	Lie down on frozen ground and it pulls heat out of you far faster than cold air does.
    31	Air barely touches you.
    32	The ground holds your whole back, and it never warms up.
    33	Nobody lies flat on that and gets up in the morning.
    34	So nobody did.
    35	There was always something in between, every night, replaced when it rotted or got wet.
    36	Grass, leaves, hide, whatever was there.
    37	The thing doing the work in that pile of grass is not the grass.
    38	It is the air trapped between the stems, which makes heat much slower to travel down into the ground.
    39	Tonight there is a whole layer under your back doing exactly that, and you call it a mattress.
    40	It was never about softness.
    41	It is about keeping the ground from reaching you.
    42	But that is reasoning.
    43	Has anyone ever dug one of these up?
    44	Yes, and the clearest one surfaced because of a drought.
    45	Through the eighties the Sea of Galilee kept dropping, from drought and from pumping, until the water sat more than six hundred feet below sea level and the old lake bed came up into the air.
    46	In 1989, on that freshly exposed mud, something appeared that had been underwater for twenty three thousand years.
    47	A camp, six huts, and it was given the name Ohalo Two.
    48	Dani Nadel and his team from the University of Haifa had about two years to dig before the water came back.
    49	Waterlogged mud is one of the very few places soft things survive, and the floor of one hut still had its bedding lying on it.
    50	Not a dark stain in the soil.
    51	Not the shadow of something that used to be there.
    52	Actual grass.
    53	Bundles of marsh grass, partly charred, pressed flat under a thin layer of clay.
    54	And they were not lying at random.
    55	Nadel recorded that they were laid out in a repeated pattern, on the floor, around a hearth in the middle.
    56	Then the water came back and covered it again.
    57	What the ground will say is that they had bedding, and that they arranged it around a fire.
    58	That the bedding was there to stop the ground from pulling heat out of them, the ground does not say.
    59	That part is still ours, laid alongside what we know about how heat moves through contact.
    60	Now the fire, and this is the part it took me longest to accept.
    61	Everyone assumes the fire is there to make the night warmer.
    62	It cannot do that.
    63	Fire does heat the air around it, that air just goes straight up and leaves, and out in the open the wind takes the rest.
    64	A heater warms a whole room, and then you forget it exists.
    65	Sit in front of a fire and your face burns while your back stays cold.
    66	What reaches your face is radiation, travelling straight from the flame to your skin, with no air carrying it.
    67	So it only reaches the side that is turned toward it.
    68	It cannot hold a room warm.
    69	It cannot hold the ground warm.
    70	What it can hold is one strip of skin.
    71	And this is where one strip of skin turns out to matter more than any of it.
    72	What predicts whether you fall asleep fast or lie there for hours is whether your body can get heat out.
    73	Not lying still.
    74	Not being tired enough.
    75	Getting rid of heat, through the skin, actively.
    76	In 2000, Kurt Kräuchi and his colleagues at the Basel psychiatric university hospital wired people up in a sleep lab and asked one question.
    77	Of everything you can measure on a person lying in the dark, what predicts how fast they fall asleep?
    78	They measured all the obvious things.
    79	Core temperature.
    80	Melatonin.
    81	Heart rate.
    82	None of those won.
    83	It was the gap between the hands and feet and the middle of the body.
    84	Not the head.
    85	The hands.
    86	You fall asleep because your core has already cooled, and the place that heat leaves from is your hands.
    87	Stick a foot out from under the covers tonight and you are doing that on purpose.
    88	Now take that same body outside on a night below freezing.
    89	The cold hits the skin and it does the oldest thing it knows how to do.
    90	It shuts the hands down.
    91	Blood is pulled out of the fingers and toes and sent to the middle, around the organs that are not allowed to cool.
    92	Hold a cold can a little too long and you have felt the first part of that.
    93	But sleep needs those vessels open.
    94	The cold just closed them.
    95	And that is why one warm strip of skin matters so much.
    96	Someone lying with one side toward the fire has exactly one patch of skin kept warm enough that the vessels there stay open.
    97	The hands can open.
    98	The heat can leave.
    99	And sleep follows.
   100	That last step is reasoning, not measurement.
   101	Nobody has ever put sensors on a person lying beside a campfire.
   102	But it is the same skin and the same vessels we are all still carrying.
   103	Except the fire gives nothing away for free.
   104	A fire far enough away to feel pleasant does nothing for your skin, and a fire close enough to warm one side of you breathes smoke into your face for as long as it burns.
   105	And you cannot move away from it, because on your other side is the cold.
   106	Warm on one side, breathing that, all night.
   107	Nobody paints that, and it happened every night.
   108	And there was one more heat source in that camp, and this one does not burn.
   109	It is the person lying next to you.
   110	A body gives off heat all night whether it wants to or not, and pressed against another body it is doing exactly what the fire does.
   111	One warm surface, against skin, and this one never goes out.
   112	Sleep alone and you get one warm side at best, and only while the fire holds.
   113	Which means the worst place in that camp is not the coldest corner.
   114	It is being on your own.
   115	And what else is out there in the dark?
   116	The cave lion.
   117	A skeleton of an adult male dug up at Siegsdorf, in Germany, stood about four feet at the shoulder, with a body close to seven feet long before the tail.
   118	Hervé Bocherens and his team read the isotopes in the bones of more than three hundred and seventy meat eaters and plant eaters across twenty five sites, to rebuild who was eating whom.
   119	For cave lions, the answer was that they did not all eat the same way.
   120	Some of them lean heavily toward reindeer.
   121	Others carry the signature of young cave bears.
   122	The isotopes do not tell you how it got hold of that bear cub.
   123	They only tell you the cub went through a lion.
   124	Nobody knows what hours it hunted.
   125	That is not the frightening part.
   126	The frightening part is that a sleeping person does not know where it is.
   127	And that camp still has to sleep.
   128	Then comes the stretch where the wood loaded at dusk has mostly burned through.
   129	The flame drops down to coals.
   130	A hide does not switch off halfway through the night.
   131	A fire does.
   132	Nobody wrote that night down, but heat moves through skin in one particular way, and if that night ran the way heat runs, then what happens next is this.
   133	When it really goes, the warm side cools within minutes, the vessels close, and the ring of people around it wakes up shivering before anyone understands why.
   134	Except one.
   135	That one was lying on the outer edge of the warm zone, the part that shrinks first as the flame drops, so the cold reaches them before it reaches anyone else.
   136	And there is one place in the ground where the ground will say part of this out loud.
   137	In what is now Moravia there is a camp called Dolní Věstonice, about twenty six thousand years old, and the houses there have hearths built inside them.
   138	Those hearths are not one fire.
   139	They are layers.
   140	Ash on charcoal on burnt bone, the same spot lit again and again.
   141	What the ground tells us for certain is only that much, that the spot was lit a great many times, over a great many years.
   142	It does not tell us who lit it, at what hour, or whether anyone was assigned to.
   143	The rest is reasoning, and I am saying so plainly.
   144	But a fire does not burn until morning on its own.
   145	So if it was still going at dawn, then someone got up in the night, in the dark, in a place that killed people.
   146	The question is who.
   147	Nothing in the ground answers that, but something alive today answers part of it.
   148	In 2017, David Samson and his team put movement trackers on thirty three Hadza people in Tanzania and watched twenty nights.
   149	Across all twenty nights, the total time when everyone wearing a tracker was asleep at once came to eighteen minutes.
   150	Nobody had scheduled that, it was just that the clock inside people runs slightly differently at different ages.
   151	Those are living people, not people from twenty three thousand years ago.
   152	But it shows a group can always have someone awake without anyone handing out shifts.
   153	So whoever got up and fed that fire was probably not chosen.
   154	They were just already awake.
   155	Tonight nobody in your house has to get up.
   156	But cold does not have to reach freezing to wreck a night.
   157	In 1981, a team led by Haskell had six men sleep in nothing but shorts on a nylon mesh frame, in a room they could set to any temperature.
   158	They tried five temperatures.
   159	The one that broke sleep hardest was seventy degrees.
   160	Room temperature.
   161	Roughly what people set a bedroom to and then never think about again.
   162	The difference is not the number seventy.
   163	The difference is that you are lying under something.
   164	A blanket does hold heat in, that much is true.
   165	But the part worth noticing is not the heat it keeps.
   166	It is where it lets the heat go, out of your core and into your skin.
   167	It puts a warm surface against you, the vessels in your skin agree to open, and the core inside you gets to cool.
   168	Warm on the outside so you can cool on the inside.
   169	A blanket is a campfire, folded up.
   170	Except this one never goes out.
   171	And that is all the evidence there is.
   172	Nobody recorded that night, all we have is grass bedding, layers of ash, and living bodies measured in a lab and in a camp in Tanzania.
   173	The sharpest number in this whole video runs on six people.
   174	Six.
   175	What the ground tells us is where they lay, not how they slept.
   176	But where they lay, it turns out, is most of the story.
   177	Because this is where the familiar picture falls apart.
   178	Everyone carries the same image of these people, huddled around a fire because they were cold.
   179	It is the other way around.
   180	They were not huddling, they were positioned.
   181	One strip of skin toward the fire.
   182	Another person against the back.
   183	A layer of grass against the ground.
   184	The three hold each other up, and take one away and the other two carry more.
   185	What kept them alive that night was not an object.
   186	Not the fire, not the hide, not the cave.
   187	It was the way those three were arranged around each other.
   188	It is what appears when you set three pieces of evidence side by side, not something anybody dug up whole.
   189	That was not surviving a night.
   190	That was running one.
   191	Now back to where we started.
   192	The hands.
   193	Tonight, when you lie down, the skin of your hands usually warms while the core inside you cools.
   194	You will not feel it.
   195	Nobody ever does.
   196	And on the nights it refuses to happen, you lie there exhausted and wide awake, with no idea why.
   197	The person lying beside that fire twenty three thousand years ago was waiting on the same thing.
   198	The difference is that they had to build a world that would let it happen.
   199	And that world asked for nine separate things, packed into one night.
   200	None of them went away.
   201	They just got handed out to a wall, a roof, a mattress, a blanket, and a box on the wall.
   202	Twenty three thousand years, from a ring of grass around a hearth to a room that handles all nine.
   203	It is nearly light.
   204	Someone turns over.
   205	The cold side goes toward the coals, the warm side goes into the dark.
   206	The shivering stops.
   207	And the hand that stayed shut all night slowly opens.
